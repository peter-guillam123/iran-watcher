What this tells us

This is the first comparison where the structural asymmetry between the two products is the dominant story. ISW's special report does one thing very well: a narrow, deeply-sourced read of the Hormuz situation drawing on Windward AI maritime tracking, Lloyd's List, UKMTO advisories, and analyst-grade Forbes/WSJ reporting. On that narrow lane they have specifics we can't match — the Hui Chuan's flag and exact location, the cumulative 70-vessels-redirected tally since 13 April, the Forbes/WSJ scoop on PRC arms-supply chains through Africa.

Our advantage here is breadth and primary-source weight. The 27-nation Multinational Military Mission joint statement is on our page as a direct gov.uk document, linked. We have IRIB's own crowing about Japan's permitted transit — corroborating ISW's "exempting friendly states" thesis from the Iranian side, in the regime's own words. We have the full Araghchi-at-BRICS diplomatic cycle, Pezeshkian's domestic-resilience messaging, Ayatollah Arafi's religious-establishment framing on the Strait. Plus the 10 UK Parliament Q&As, the €50m bounty bill, the Yemen prisoner exchange, Hezbollah's northern strike, the domestic war-economy crackdown — none of which appear in ISW's special report at all (their separate daily Iran Update would cover them, but this report is narrow by design).

The honest summary: for the Hormuz lane on 14 May, the case is to read both. For everything else on 14 May, ours is doing what ISW's special report explicitly isn't trying to do.

What this comparison most clearly identifies as a future gap: a maritime-OSINT layer. Vessel-level tracking (Windward AI, MarineTraffic) and the Lloyd's List subscription wire would close the gap on named-incident reporting like Hui Chuan. ReliefWeb was the last named gap from the v1 scoping document; maritime-OSINT is now the most obvious v2 candidate, especially because Hormuz is structurally the dominant theme of this phase of the conflict.
